Europe keeps arguing about the retirement age, but the age only tells you when you are allowed to stop, not how long you get afterwards. This chart projects that second number: for someone entering the workforce now, the gap between the pension age today's law sets for them and the age they are expected to live to.

| | Longest retirement | Shortest retirement |
|---|---|---|
| Men | Luxembourg 26.0 · Slovenia 25.3 · Switzerland 24.1 | Denmark 16.1 · Estonia 17.6 · Slovakia 19.1 |
| Women | Poland 29.8 · Luxembourg 29.4 · Slovenia 29.0 | Denmark 18.8 · Estonia 21.1 · Cyprus 21.9 |

## The formula countries

Ten countries no longer set a fixed pension age; they tie it to life expectancy, and six of them hold the shortest projected retirements in Europe. Raising a fixed pension age costs a government a vote that tends to make headlines. A formula avoids that vote: the age climbs on its own as lives get longer, and the risk of living longer shifts from the state to the worker. Denmark, with a projected pension age of 74, is the clearest case.

## These are not today's pension ages

Readers who checked the chart against their own country's pension age today often found a different number. That is expected. The pension ages here are OECD projections of current legislation for someone entering the labour market at 22 in 2024, so they include every increase already written into law. Life expectancy is Eurostat's projection for the year each cohort reaches that age, measured at the pension age rather than at birth.

## What the chart leaves out

The pension age is a legal threshold, not a description of how people actually live: many leave work earlier through early retirement, disability or unemployment, while others carry on past it. Length is not the whole story either, since a year in poor health counts the same here as a year in good health. And decades of pension reform still sit between these workers and their retirement, so this is what the law promises them today, not a forecast of what they will receive.
